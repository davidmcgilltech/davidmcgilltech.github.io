What Website Maintenance Actually Covers

  • Home

Most small businesses pay for website maintenance without ever seeing a list of what it includes. The invoice arrives, the site looks the same as last month, and nobody can say what the money bought. Here is what the line item should actually cover.

Security and dependency updates

Every website runs on software written by other people, and that software gets security patches. A WordPress site with an out-of-date plugin is the single most common way a small business site gets defaced or turned into a spam relay. Real maintenance means those updates get applied and tested on a schedule, not when something breaks.

Backups you have actually restored

A backup nobody has ever restored is a hope, not a backup. Ask your provider when they last performed a test restore. If the answer is that they have never done one, you do not have backups. You have files.

Uptime and error monitoring

You should not be the one who discovers your site is down, and you should certainly not discover it from a customer. Monitoring means an automated check runs every few minutes and someone gets paged when it fails.

Content changes

New hours, a new staff photo, an updated price list. Many plans quietly cap this or bill it separately at an hourly rate. Get the number of included changes in writing.

Broken link and form checks

Contact forms fail silently more often than anything else on a small business site. An email provider changes a setting, a spam filter starts eating submissions, and you lose leads for months without a single error message. Forms should be tested on a schedule by submitting them.

What to ask before you sign

  1. What specifically is included, in a written list?
  2. Who owns the site, the domain, and the hosting account? If the answer is not you, that is the real problem, not the maintenance fee.
  3. What happens to the site if I cancel?
  4. When was the last test restore of my backups?
  5. Is there a response time commitment when something breaks?
